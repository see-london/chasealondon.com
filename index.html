Showing dealers what the program is worth

Trane Technologies · built 2018–2021, rebuilt 2024–present
Problem

Dealers pay to join the dealer program, and account managers need to show them what they get back. That answer was spread across a dozen sources, and the monthly update took days of manual work with close attention to detail.

What I did

I built the first scrappy version in 2018 with Excel, Alteryx and Tableau. In 2024 I took it back and rebuilt it: 4 internal cloud sources plus 8–10 partner feeds, cleaned and joined into one monthly dataset on program use, purchases and ROI for every dealer. I also set up automated national-comparison reports for independent distributors who don't have access to our internal dashboards.

Result

A 3,000–5,000-dealer network in one interactive dashboard. Account, territory and regional managers use it to run their regions and in one-on-one reviews to renew dealers and show them where to improve. The monthly refresh went from days of manual work to about 90% automated.

Built to last

The old version was a separate table for every source, rebuilt each year, so it could only ever show one year. I restructured everything around a single dealer source of truth and a date key. Every source now lands in one table, and each month's data is appended instead of rebuilt, so the dashboard runs year over year. It works at the month level; managers asked for year-to-date, so that's what it shows, and switching back is easy.

A clear picture of a church's finances

Volunteer Finance Chair · local church · 2024–present
Problem

The church's numbers lived in dense accounting exports. Leaders knew money was tight, but there was no shared picture of why, or how long the reserves would last.

What I did

Turned the exports into a simple monthly report and a three-chart briefing for church leadership: who's attending, where the money goes, and what happens if nothing changes.

Result

Everyone now works from the same facts. Spending runs under budget, but giving doesn't cover it, so the church draws on its reserves every month. At the current gap, those reserves last about 7 years.

What I'm changing

I can't control attendance, but I can make every dollar clearer. For the 2027 budget I'm leading an overhaul: a cleaner set of account codes, a real approval process, budgets built around programs instead of staff positions, and training so staff and volunteers can check their own budgets and file reports themselves.

Reporting that runs itself

Trane Technologies · 2024–present
Problem

Leadership decisions across 6 brands and 4 business units depended on recurring reports that took hands-on steps every cycle: pulling exports, rerunning steps and checking results. As the need for data grew, there was a real opportunity to make them faster, more reliable and easier to scale.

What I did

I mapped how every input would show up in the output leaders needed, found the manual steps and failure points, and redesigned the process end to end: automated file collection, repeatable prep, scheduled publishing, and automated data checks I keep adding to as new issues appear. I led my team's move to a more stable platform and, as part of Trane's Dataiku Champions group, taught the team and was their go-to contact while they learned. Tools: Dataiku, Power Automate, Python.

Result

20+ pipelines I build and maintain now feed 15+ leadership and decision dashboards on schedule. The team gets back dozens of hours a month (about 5 a week for me), and some monthly projects went from days of manual work to hands-off runs.

Framework I created · Levels of Automation

A shared scale for "how good is this data source?"

When I started this role, most of marketing didn't have a way to talk about data sources beyond "it works" or "it's a pain." Borrowing from the levels used for self-driving cars, I built a 0–5 scale that grades every source by how much a person still has to do to use it. It gave the team a common language for where each source stands, what the next step is and where to invest.

Grade a source

A source's level is the lowest level it fully meets on all four questions. The weakest step sets the grade.

    Tuning the dealer locator

    Magenta Technologies (a Trane venture) · 2022–2024
    Problem

    Homeowners use the dealer locator to find someone to call. Distance was a good start, but there was room to put the best-fit dealer first, and the data suggested lots of repeated searching.

    What I did

    Designed a dealer score from customer feedback, response speed, Google reviews and dealer level, so the best-fit dealer shows first in each ZIP code. Then I dug into how people actually use the tool and found the site was logging a search on every page load, so thousands of those repeat searches were page loads, not people.

    Result

    Simpler ranking logic and accurate reporting. It also set the locator strategy: always show three choices, and when no dealer is available, a general contact card takes the empty spot. That was tested and is live today.

      Fewer calls, better leads

      Magenta Technologies (a Trane venture) · 2022–2024
      Problem

      To connect website visits to dealer phone calls, every session gets its own tracking number. We moved from a vendor pool of millions of numbers to our own Twilio setup with only a few thousand that rotate. Call volume dropped hard, and the question was whether we'd lost real customers.

      What I did

      The rotation logic was built by developers; I owned the analysis. I compared call patterns, durations and completion before and after the switch to find out who the "lost" callers actually were.

      Result

      Total calls fell about 80%, but wins held flat. The lost calls were almost all bots, random dials and people who weren't looking for a dealer, and every step of the funnel now converts better. Average dealer call time went from under 30 seconds to about a minute. It changed how the business defines a healthy call volume.

      A BTU calculator for homeowners

      Magenta Technologies (a Trane venture) · 2022–2024
      Problem

      Most homeowners have no idea what size system they need, and that uncertainty stalls them before they ever call a dealer. The official load calculations need details a shopper doesn't have.

      What I did

      Researched how dealers size systems, then pushed to get real-world housing listing data with known system types. I used machine learning to weight each factor (square footage, ceilings, insulation, windows, orientation, occupants, age) into a points system. Homeowners answer in simple ranges instead of exact numbers. The output was good, better and best options (14, 17 and 20 SEER) with a cost-to-own chart using local weather and energy prices, plus a call-a-dealer button.

      Result

      The proof of concept became the base developers built the website tool from. I've rebuilt my version as a working calculator on this site.

      Looking past the first result

      Magenta Technologies (a Trane venture) · 2021–2022
      Problem

      We ran dozens of tests a year, but campaign traffic was thin, so down-funnel results were sparse and often inconclusive. One test removed a "contact dealer now" card from product category pages. It showed no clear impact either way, so the card came out.

      What I did

      About a month later, leads from that area were way down. I went through the lead data and found that the source had dropped out as expected, but those calls never showed up anywhere else. Visitors weren't finding another path to a dealer.

      Result

      We brought the card back and lead volume recovered. The lesson I carry: when traffic is thin, keep watching the downstream numbers after anything ships.
